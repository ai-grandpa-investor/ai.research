Alphabet without the Anthropic windfall: what the business earns, and what the price pays for it

Alphabet (GOOG)

Inconclusive

The short version

Most of the profit Alphabet reported in the first half of 2026 did not come from Google. Of $14.24 a share, $8.96 came from marking up the value of stakes it holds in other companies. $7.48 of that came from private stakes, which Alphabet's quarterly report says are mostly one private company. The report does not name it; public reports say it is the AI company Anthropic. Listed shares, SpaceX among them, added $1.42.

Take those gains out and the business earned $5.28 a share, up 21% on a year earlier. Analysts expect about $11.65 for the whole of 2026 on that basis, and $15.14 in 2027. At the close of 5 October 2026, $343.83, the shares cost 29.5 times the business's 2026 earnings and 22.7 times 2027's.

The business is growing fast. Revenue rose 24.2% in the second quarter of 2026, against 11% to 15% a year in the two years to mid-2025, and analysts expect it to stay above that old range until early 2028.

Why it matters

A gain on a stake is real wealth, but it is not a profit the business makes again next year. It comes when a private company raises money at a higher price, or a listed one's shares rise, and it can reverse. A P/E on reported earnings mixes the two: on 2026 it looks like 16.7 times, which is the business plus a windfall. On the business alone it is 29.5 times.

A fixed rule, the same for every company we look at, says the faster pace began in the third quarter of 2025: four quarters in a row are outside the range of the eight before. Measured against the last eight reported quarters, that range is 13.2% to 17.8%, and the forecasts come back inside it only in the second quarter of 2028.

What the filings do not say

How much of Google Cloud's revenue comes from Anthropic or other AI companies Alphabet has invested in. Alphabet does not report revenue by customer, so that part cannot be split out. The filing gives only the whole: Google Cloud's revenue rose to $24.8 billion in the second quarter of 2026 from $13.6 billion a year earlier, it began selling TPU systems that quarter, and its revenue backlog, contracts not yet recognised as revenue, stood at $513.9 billion at the end of June.

Nor can one company prove the growth rule right; that will be known only as the quarters are reported. The forecasts thin out beyond 2027, to ten analysts for the 2028 quarters. And a P/E says what the price pays for one year's forecast, not whether the forecast is right.

How it was run

Code only, no AI. We took Alphabet's revenue for every quarter since 2015 from its SEC filings and joined it to analysts' forecasts saved on 5 October 2026. One rule decides when growth leaves its usual range: a quarter is unusual when its growth on a year earlier is further from the median of the eight quarters before it than 1.5 points or 15% of that median, whichever is larger, and two unusual quarters in a row on the same side start a new pace. For forecasts the range is fixed at the last eight reported quarters, and a quarter with fewer than five analysts is not judged. The forward P/E is the closing price divided by the analysts' average earnings per share for each year, on the same accounting basis as the annual report (GAAP). The business's earnings are pre-tax income less the gains on equity securities, as filed; both parts are taxed at the period's effective rate, because the filing gives no tax on the gains alone.

Question
How much of Alphabet's profit comes from its business, and how much from revaluing its stakes in other companies? How long do analysts expect its faster growth to last, and what does the share price pay for the business's earnings?
Hypothesis
The filings are enough to split Alphabet's earnings into the business and the stakes, and one fixed rule, the same for every company, can read from the filings and the analysts' forecasts when a growth spurt began and when analysts expect it to end.
